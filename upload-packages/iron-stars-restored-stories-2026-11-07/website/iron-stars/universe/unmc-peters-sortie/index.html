UNMC — Peter’s Sortie

Peter fights through a ruined city while every damaged actuator adds to his debt.

United Nations Megacorporations (UNMC) / Cybernetic Rebellion

Peter slid in behind the bot flyer and held his breath, waiting for the green bite of target lock. When it came, he dumped his last missiles.

They streaked true—until the flyer spewed a cloud of anti-missile gnats. His warheads vanished into twinkling blossoms of counterfire.

He could already hear Brass in his skull: Each one of those missiles costs more than you make in a year, flyboy.
“Dammit,” he hissed, hauling the reticle across the flyer’s jittering cone of evasive thrust. He snapped an angry laser shot anyway and watched it carve empty sky.

Remember: the bots are logical to a fault. They don’t account for human stupidity.

He stopped chasing. Killed the forward vector. Thumbed the hybrid toggle.

Actuators howled as the frame shifted: wings folding, legs half-deployed, back thrusters yawning open. He kicked the retro pack and yanked the stick against his own instincts, throwing himself backward into his own wake. The flyer’s path prediction overshot; for one clean instant Peter’s firing lane ran straight as a spear.

He cursed for luck and hosed the flyer with laser fire. The beam stitched through its sensor head; the bot jerked, tried to correct, then flamed out and fell like bad math.

Now gravity wanted its cut. He was dropping fast, airspeed shedding in ugly chunks. No missiles left meant airspace was his only until the next problem; airspace without ordnance just made him a bigger target.

He switched fully to mech mode and rode the descent on screaming ankles, hit the street hard enough to shake grit down the canopy, then let the frame settle into a crouch. Somewhere above, the sky kept the low thunder of distant fights. Down here the city breathed smoke and static.

He flexed the right arm. The elbow actuator complained with a long, hungry creak.

“Cheap megacorp scrap,” he muttered. “Not what they keep for their golden boys.” He pictured the slick vids: Become an elite pilot—luxury, respect, the good life. Sure, he was “elite” inside the UNMC, but compared to New Avalon Knights? He’d seen a Knight once—more dance than duel. When it ended, a battalion of mechs lay in glittering pieces around a machine that barely looked winded.

Not me, he thought. Not with this kit. Not with these debts.

He rolled the frame into shadow and started moving, low and quiet. He’d dropped behind enemy lines; that meant a flank if he played it right. It also meant every bot on this grid would expect exactly that.

He rounded a corner—and there it was, a Rebel scout already sighting on where his cockpit should have been. Peter was already sliding, frame low along its right hip, left foot skidding sparks. He put his sights on the CPU core and tapped, once, twice. The bot’s head blinked out; the body took one blind step and fell.

The elbow creaked again. Another line on the maintenance sheet. Another invoice. Another bite of his life.

He heard his old XO like a ghost in the cockpit: That’s how they get you. You try to stay alive, you patch what breaks, and suddenly you’ve got debts you can’t repay and a lifetime of servitude to the bastards who sold you the dream.

Peter stood the mech and checked lanes. Target glyphs winked and crawled across the HUD—bots dug in along avenues, perched in blown-out windows, nested in tram stations. Plenty of work. Plenty of risk. Plenty of ways to end the day deeper in the red.

He breathed out through his teeth. “Every bot downed is a payment,” he said, making it real by saying it. “I’m behind. Gotta finish the day in the green.”

He moved.

He kept the frame light-footed despite its protest, using debris for cover, hugging the dead angles the bots’ logic trees habitually missed. Twice he let a patrol pass, counting their spacing, then cut in to erase the tail unit and fade again before the front two realized the math didn’t add. He baited a sniper line with a heat flare, then looped a service alley and put his laser through the tower’s knee, toppling the perch and the ugly geometry of its kill zone.

He avoided big plays. Big plays meant big repairs, which meant big bills. He trimmed and trimmed—one more bot erased, one more lane turned safe for the poor souls cowering in basements he’d never see.

At an intersection blown to rebar and dust, he paused long enough to watch his HUD calculate the day’s ugliness in terse numerals. Armor wear. Actuator fatigue. Cooling gel below optimal. Laser aperture drift creeping into the yellow. Each warning was a hand in his pocket.

A trio of bots pushed a block ahead, metal feet ticking like metronomes over tile. He set his jaw and slid into their blind arc, counting heartbeats to keep the panic small. First shot took the lead’s optic. Second shot cored the second’s logic box as it tried to reassign itself as leader. The third hesitated a fraction too long—logic asking permission from logic—while Peter vaulted a shattered kiosk and buried a beam into its chest.

He landed hard. The elbow squealed again. He winced and rolled his shoulder inside the suit like it might help.

“Almost there,” he told no one. “Almost even.”

The city answered with distant sirens, the crackle of burning wires, and the hollow wind that cities make when they’re scared. He checked the map ghosts of UNMC lines—thin, hungry things—and the thicker, healthier lines of New Avalon territory to the west, bright as a promise he could never collect.

Peter leveled his laser toward the next knot of red triangles and started walking.

Maybe—maybe—he could end this day in the green. And if not today, then tomorrow. If not tomorrow, then the day after. Keep moving. Keep paying. Keep breathing.

That was the job. That was the life they’d sold him. And until the debt was ashes, it was the only way through.
