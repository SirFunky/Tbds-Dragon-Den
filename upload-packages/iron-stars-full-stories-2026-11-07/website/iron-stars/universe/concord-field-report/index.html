Iron Concord — Yelena’s Escape

Lt. Yelena Markovic returns from the Neutral Zone with a damaged mech and a long memory.

Iron Concord

Yelena drove her scout mech through the dense forest, shin plates shouldering aside trunks that blurred past like prison bars. Behind her, the trees burst into splinters under the Cybernetic Rebellion’s retaliatory fire—white-hot lances that turned sap to steam and bark to ash.

She’d been point for the Concord strike that burned down one of the Rebels’ manufacturing nodes. They didn’t forgive that kind of wound. They hunted.

Her comms were drowned in static—broadband jam, tightband hiss. All she had left was dead reckoning and the last known ping for the extraction site.

A laser burned past her canopy, a straight edge of light that nipped a sensor vane and filled the cockpit with scorched-plastic stink.

“Frack! How did they get ahead of me so fast?” she snapped, yanking the frame into a hard left around a ridge of granite.

“They were already there. Also—no one’s waiting at your extraction,” a voice crackled over the short-band like broken glass.

Her teeth clenched. Not a hack—wrong cadence. Braze. The Brass Division loved to sandbag scouts. “Copy,” she muttered to herself, because there was no one else to hear it. Pinched between two Rebel elements, ten miles of Neutral Zone between her and the nearest Concord base.

Fine. First: shake the tail.

She slid the mech behind a boulder’s shadow, killed external lights, throttled the reactor down to a whisper. The forest held its breath with her. A silver-bodied scout bot sprinted past, optics hot and blind to anything not on its optimal pathing.

Yelena powered up on the offbeat. The rig hummed awake. She leveled her forearm lasers, cut deep across the bot’s knee actuators, and it folded like wet wire. She was moving again before the wreck finished falling—back into the trees, into the stink of sap and smoke, heartbeat in her ears like a metronome set too fast.

“They hate illogic,” she reminded herself. “Use that.”

Another mile. Another bend.

This time, she kept the power signatures live. The pursuing bots ghosted in on her beacon, came wide around the rock with guns already chattering—exactly as she needed. Only the mech they expected to be standing was pressed flat against the earth, profile low and weapons already set on the angle of their CPU housings.

Yelena pulsed the lasers: two short, surgical bursts. The lead bot went dead, chest smoking. The second tripped over it, momentum carrying it snout-first into the dirt. Yelena kicked her scout upright, vaulted the heap, and used it like a springboard—boots slamming onto the third bot’s shoulders as she cleared it. She gave it a contemptuous heel-kick across the head casing on her way past.

“Two still on me,” she said, mostly to pin the thought down. Adrenaline made the world too bright, too loud.

A trick wouldn’t work twice. She needed terrain.

Memory flashed the briefing map across her mind: a lake ahead, long and narrow, fed by a mountain run. Bad place to fight. Great place to disappear.

She eased the throttle, let her mech’s wake fall calm, then walked it backward down the shelf and into the lake, one careful step at a time. Dark water climbed the canopy. She cut the reactor to bare idle and lay the scout on its back under the surface, heat sinks sipping cold.

Minutes stretched. Then the pair of Rebel scouts tore past on the shore, their logic trees pruning away “pilot hides in lake” as too stupid to model.

Yelena counted to sixty, then another sixty for luck, and pulsed the reactor. Systems came up in a low growl. She stood the mech, water sheeting from the armor—

—and froze. On the ridge above the lake, perched like a carrion bird, a Rebel flyer sat with its nose pointed square at her cockpit. Its wing lasers winked alive.

She didn’t have time to curse.

The flyer’s head erupted in a spray of parts. The body hung stupidly for a heartbeat, then toppled from the rock and vanished into the trees with a crash that shook the water at her knees.

Yelena didn’t look for the savior. Miracles are for after-action reports. She ran—south, away from the scouts, away from the ridge gun that should have killed her. Branches clawed her hull; warning glyphs came and went; she ignored the ones that didn’t mean “dead in two seconds.”

Hours later—blood dried tacky under the hardsuit, teeth aching from clench—she crossed the Neutral Zone boundary beacons and came up on the Concord outpost.

Abandoned.

Gates yawning. Towers cold. Only the wind moving, and a paper ops notice flapping itself to death on a chain-link fence. Brass had pulled out. No word. No pickup. No nothing.

She stood in the empty access lane and let the anger land. Not hot. Not childish. Knife-clean.

“Whoever signed this op,” Yelena said softly, “I’ll fry you for it.”

She turned her scout toward home. The forest behind her still burned. The Neutral Zone ahead was a pale, empty line. Somewhere out there, an unseen shooter had spared her. Somewhere closer, a Concord commander had spent her like ammo.

She would remember both.
