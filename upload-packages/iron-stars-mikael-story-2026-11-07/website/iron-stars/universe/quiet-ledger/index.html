Operation: Quiet Ledger

Black Tern Recovery & Security accepts a New Avalon contract to blind a UNMC relay station for eight minutes.

Southern Cross Compact / New Avalon / United Nations Megacorporations (UNMC)

By that stage of the war, there were not many green pilots left.

The dead had taken most of them. The smart ones had become instructors. Everyone still climbing into a cockpit for pay had seen enough fire to know that glory was a bad investment.

Rook Mercer knew that before the contract ever hit his screen.

Employer: New Avalon, through three shell accounts and one very polite intermediary.
Objective: blind a PanOptik relay station for eight minutes.
Opposition: UNMC security detachment.
Payment: excellent.
Survival odds: not mentioned.

Their company, Black Tern Recovery & Security, took the job anyway.

Not because they believed in New Avalon. Southern Cross didn’t do belief. It did signatures, deposits, and exit plans.

Rook’s squad rolled toward the relay through the ruins of an old mining settlement half-buried under slag, shattered conveyors, and collapsed hab-blocks. Four machines, all stock, all hard-used, all ugly in different ways.

Captain Sera Vale led in an old Iron Concord Legate Frame called Old Law—thick armor, command suite still functional, one shoulder patched with scavenged plate that didn’t quite match the rest.
Jo Kassian followed in a UNDF Aegis-Line Frame named Ledger, reliable as a hammer.
Ash Drennan skittered ahead in a Surveyor-5 Crawler called Tick, all sensors and bad attitude.
And Rook flew overwatch in a salvaged UNDF Interceptor, Magpie, its sponsor markings sanded off years ago and replaced with Southern Cross hazard stripes.

“Remember the job,” Sera said over squad comms. “We’re not here to beat the UNMC. We’re here to make them blind and angry for eight minutes.”

Ash snorted. “That all?”

Jo added, “Blind, angry, and invoiced.”

That got a laugh, thin and practiced.

Ahead of them, the relay station sat on a ridge like a polished knife. PanOptik screens wrapped the tower in mirrored light. Below it, the UNMC security detachment waited in textbook formation.

Tick painted them one by one.

“Contact,” Ash said. “One Hammernet. One Aegis-Line. Two Interceptors. And they’ve got their own crawler tucked under the west gantry.”

Rook exhaled slowly.

That was better.

That was believable.

A real fight.

Not good odds, but mercenary odds.

Sera’s voice stayed calm. “Tick, keep their spotter honest. Ledger, with me through the slag terraces. Magpie, screen the air and kill anything that tries to flank.”

Rook rolled Magpie into Flight Mode and skimmed low over the ridge shadow. Immediately the enemy knew he was there.

The first missile pass came in from the right—one of the UNMC Interceptors dropping through cloud like a spear. Rook broke hard, missiles chasing heat and empty air, then snapped back and returned fire with a burst of micro-swarms that forced the other pilot high.

Not a kill. Just pressure.

That was how veterans fought. Not for spectacle. For inches.

Below him, Tick and the enemy crawler opened their own private war. Sensor pings, spoof packets, autocannon bursts into cover. Neither wanted the other dead as badly as they wanted the other blind.

Then the Hammernet woke up.

It fired once, and half the ridge vanished in smoke.

Quad cannons hammered the slag terraces where Sera and Jo were advancing. The blasts were accurate enough to feel personal. The UNMC Aegis-Line moved up behind that fire, disciplined and neat, while the second Interceptor cut low to support.

“Contact lane locked!” Ash barked. “Their crawler’s feeding the Hammernet.”

“Can you break it?” Sera asked.

“Working on it.”

Rook saw the problem immediately. The UNMC line was doing what it was built to do—spotter marks the target, Hammernet cripples it, fast units exploit the damage. A clean corporate kill-chain. Efficient. Branded. Deadly.

Southern Cross had no doctrine to answer that.

So they answered it with experience.

Sera drove Old Law straight into a collapsed ore chute that should have slowed a heavier machine to a crawl. Instead she used the broken ground like she knew every stone by name, climbing through debris at an angle the UNMC line clearly hadn’t expected. Jo followed tighter behind, Ledger taking the safer route but keeping line of sight.

“Rook,” Sera said, “push the right Interceptor. Keep him off Jo.”

“On it.”

Magpie screamed over the ridge in a low pass. The enemy Interceptor turned to meet him, and for three seconds it was just two old machines trying to kill each other over a graveyard of concrete and rust.

Rook took a rail shot across the shoulder. The enemy answered with missiles that tore armor off Magpie’s left wing. Warning runes flared across his cockpit. He ignored them.

Below, Tick finally won the sensor duel.

“Tagged their crawler,” Ash said. “Now, now, now—”

Jo fired first. Ledger’s coilgun punched through the gantry supports. Sera’s Legate followed with suppressive fire that chewed the crawler’s cover into scrap. Then Rook dropped a missile spread into the breach.

The enemy spotter vanished in fire.

For one heartbeat, the UNMC line faltered.

That was all veterans needed.

Sera drove Old Law out of the chute and into the Hammernet’s flank before it could fully retask. Not a duel. Not heroics. Just a brutal side-angle attack into a weak point opened by timing and bad terrain.

Jo hit the same target a second later.

The Hammernet staggered, one missile rack blowing outward in a shower of white-hot fragments.

Then the UNMC Aegis-Line hit back.

It came through the smoke and rammed Ledger hard enough to knock the two machines into the bones of an old conveyor tower. Jo and the UN pilot vanished into close-range plasma flashes and grinding armor. No speeches. No threats. Just two professionals trying to put steel through the other one first.

Rook lined up another attack run on the damaged Hammernet, but the surviving Interceptor came in on his tail again. He had no choice but to break off.

“Magpie, where are you?” Sera snapped.

“Busy not dying!”

“Do it later.”

The Hammernet fired again.

This time it hit Tick.

Ash’s Crawler vanished behind a flash of impact and a plume of black dust. His signal dipped, came back, dipped again.

“Ash?”

Static.

Then, faintly: “Still here. Lost a leg. Sensor mast’s half gone.” A wet cough over the comm, though Rook knew perfectly well Ash was nowhere near anything wet. “Relay’s still live. Want me to keep feeding?”

Sera did not answer immediately.

That told them everything.

Ash laughed once, tired and ugly. “Yeah. Thought so.”

Tick dragged itself higher onto the ridge, broken chassis scraping over slag, and forced its last clean uplink through. Enemy positions lit across every cockpit in the squad.

Rook used the feed first.

Magpie transformed, dove, and emptied everything he had left into the relay tower itself. Missiles tore open one side of the PanOptik arrays. His rail carbine lanced through a sensor spine. Screens burst into static.

Jo finished the enemy Aegis-Line with a plasma blade through the torso and stumbled free of the wreck with Ledger’s right arm hanging useless.

Sera put Old Law into the Hammernet one last time at point-blank range.

The heavy mech went down ugly—no grand explosion, just a collapse of armor, sparks, and failing hydraulics as a machine too expensive to die did it anyway.

Then the surviving UNMC Interceptor broke away.

Not routed. Not panicked. Just beaten enough to know the contract was lost.

Professional to the end.

The relay tower burned. PanOptik lightboards flickered, glitched, and died. For eight minutes the ridge went blind.

Ash never answered another comm.

By the time Black Tern pulled back through the mining ruins, Magpie was bleeding fuel, Ledger had one arm left, and Old Law looked like it had been dug out of a landfill under artillery fire.

They still brought home three pilots.

And when the New Avalon intermediary met them at the fallback strip, clean coat, clean boots, not one speck of battlefield ash on him, he asked only one question.

“Was the window opened?”

Sera looked back at the covered salvage tarp where Tick’s black box sat beside what was left of Ash Drennan’s helmet.

Then she held out her datapad.

“For eight minutes and nineteen seconds,” she said. “Longer than contracted.”

The intermediary checked the numbers. “New Avalon appreciates precision.”

Sera’s face did not change.

“Southern Cross appreciates amended hazard pay.”

He looked at the ruin of their machines, at the scorch marks, at the recovery crews already cutting useful parts off UNMC wrecks in the background.

Then, very carefully, he nodded.

Rook watched him authorize the transfer.

That was the thing outsiders never understood about the Compact.

They thought mercenaries were cowards. Opportunists. Scavengers circling real armies.

Sometimes they were right.

But sometimes a contract lance of old survivors could do what proud nations could not: go where the job was filthy, do it without banners, lose one of their own, and still leave carrying both the dead and the invoice.

That was not glory.

That was Southern Cross.

And in the Iron Stars, it was often enough.
