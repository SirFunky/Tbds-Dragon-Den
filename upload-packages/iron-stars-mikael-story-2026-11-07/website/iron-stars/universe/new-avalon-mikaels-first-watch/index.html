New Avalon — Mikael’s First Watch

On his first watch, Mikael faces a Concord attack after Sir Kael’s frame falls.

New Avalon / Iron Concord

Mikael’s first hour as a knight had been all ceremony—oaths spoken beneath cold lights, his hands shaking inside the neural cuffs. His second hour was smoke and screaming metal.

The wreck of Sir Kael’s frame lay twisted across the avenue like a gutted cathedral: scorched armor plates, a sparking spine of cabling, the visor split open. Mikael crouched behind a half-collapsed office block, his Knight-Errant’s servos idling low, shield array folded tight against his arm. The air tasted of ozone and rebar dust. Every breath in his cockpit suit came ragged, loud as thunder inside his own head.

An hour ago, he’d felt only excitement when the alert chimed. Now he felt fear, clean and absolute.

Is this how I die? On my first watch, under a sky that doesn’t know my name?

He swallowed it. He had a duty. New Avalon didn’t raise knights to count heartbeats and freeze.

A Concord scout strode into view at the far end of the street—light frame, boxy shoulders, the ugly precision of stamped steel—its optics sweeping where Kael’s body lay. The Iron Concord’s barrage still hammered the district from somewhere beyond the rail viaduct; each impact shook pulverized glass into glittering rain.

Mikael slid his rifle up from the Knight’s back mount. The neural rig threaded calm across his vision: range ticks; wind; a thin, blue aim-line. He exhaled, let the crosshair settle by instinct more than thought, and touched the trigger.

The laser carved a white line through dust and distance. The scout’s visor bloomed into liquid metal. The machine folded, smoke coiling from a cockpit that had become slag.

“Contact,” Mikael breathed to no one. The comm net was fried—Concord jammers feathered the meters with static.

The rest of the Concord lance answered at once. The street turned to starlight—red tracers, orange phosphor, needles of green that chewed masonry into powder where he had been a heartbeat earlier. Mikael threw himself behind the bent ribs of a skybridge support. Shrapnel spattered like rain against his shield.

Three left. Closing.

He mag-latched the rifle to his back. “On me,” he whispered to the dead and to himself. Shield out. Saber live.

The beam rapier flared to life with a tone he felt in his molars. He could see them moving through the gaps—two more scouts and something heavier hanging back, its silhouette fat with ammo hoppers and cooling fins.

They came fast. The first scout swept the corner, muzzle bright. Mikael stepped into it. One cut, clean as a thought—ankle to shoulder. The upper half of the mech slid away, cauterized, the legs still taking two blind steps before they toppled.

The heavy fired. The world lurched white.

Impact hammered his shield, forced it down a fraction, alarms chattering. The shot would have cored his cockpit had he been half a second slower. He didn’t give the heavy a second shot. Mikael kicked the Knight’s rocket boosters, a short, savage burn that smeared the street into a grey streak. He burst into the heavy’s firing lane before it could traverse.

Up close the Iron Concord fire-support frame was a scarred beast, armored cheeks, a mouthful of cannons. He used the shield to take the first wild swing of its gun arm, turned the second aside with elbow and momentum, then drove the rapier into the mass behind its throat-louvers. The blade went in smooth, met something dense, went in anyway. He cut down, reversed, cut again. The heavy shuddered, choked light from a dozen seams, and collapsed with a crash that shivered the neighboring façades.

“One,” he said. “Two.”

His rig painted an arrow on the HUD—last contact retreating. The remaining scout had seen the heavy fall and turned tail for the horizon. It fired a spiteful burst over its shoulder, rounds striking sparks from streetcar rails as it ran.

“Coward,” Mikael muttered, and then, ashamed of the heat in his voice, “—no. Live runner means more civilians shelled tomorrow.”

He checked the wreckage where Sir Kael lay. Dust swirled. No signal response. He’d mourn later or never. Knights did not write their grief until the district was safe.

Mikael sprinted.

The Knight-Errant devoured the block with long, piston-smooth strides. He vaulted a toppled billboard, boosters kicking when his heel snagged twisted steel. The scout took the corner hard, boots shrieking across ceramic pavement, and spilled into the old tram plaza—bad ground for flyers, good ground for duels. Beyond, the viaduct climbed into a haze of gun smoke. If the scout reached that ramp, it would disappear into a web of covering fire.

“Not today.”

He took the line of approach from the memory of a drill: don’t chase the back; cut the angle. He shredded across the plaza at a diagonal, let the boosters lift him just enough to clear a kiosk husk, and came down in the scout’s path with his shield already rising.

The scout fired point-blank—too late. Shots sparked across his shield array in a strobing halo. Mikael pushed into the muzzle, turned its barrel aside with the shield’s edge, and drove his rapier through the machine’s chest. The beam hissed; something inside coupled, popped. He yanked free, swept a horizontal cut that took the mech at the hip. The top half turned once as if to look back, then crashed to the stones.

Silence, except for the stutter of distant artillery and his own ragged breathing.

Mikael stood in the center of the plaza, saber dimming to nothing. He listened for more—footfalls, the thunder of Concord linewalkers, the ripping whine of missiles. The district answered with settling dust and the thin, surprised cries of people realizing they were not dead.

He looked back down the street. Sir Kael was still a ruin of lightless steel.

“District secure,” Mikael said into the static, because someone should say it. He set the Knight’s feet, lifted the shield, and turned toward the viaduct where the barrage still stitched the clouds. Duty did not end with three kills. Duty never ended.

He began to walk.
